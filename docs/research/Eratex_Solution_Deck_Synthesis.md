## 1. Purpose of This Document

This document synthesizes the visible-slide content extracted from the Eratex Solution Deck. The deck was prepared for Eratex Djaja Tbk as a proposal for a garment manufacturing scheduling and execution-control solution.

The presentation is not only a software pitch. It is a proposed operating model for improving reliability, OTIF, plant stability, working capital, and management bandwidth through:

- Capacity-constrained planning.
- Resource-based order loading.
- Plan freeze and controlled execution.
- Priority-driven execution management.
- Workflow automation for merchandising and pre-production.
- Scheduling tools for critical operations such as laundry.
- Review dashboards and ongoing improvement routines.

Hidden slides were excluded from the extraction and are not considered here.

## 2. Presentation Thesis

The central thesis is:

Eratex can become a preferred supplier only if it delivers high OTIF consistently, on originally committed dates, within customer-accepted lead times, without relying on firefighting, overtime, excess inventory, and management follow-up.

The deck argues that Eratex already appears strong on basic on-time delivery when measured against revised ex-factory dates and with a 1-2 day leeway. However, the business is not yet reliably fulfilling the deeper customer requirement: on-time and in-full delivery against the first customer commitment, with shorter and dependable lead times.

The proposed answer is a system-driven operating model where planning and execution are governed by plant reality:

- Orders are committed only after checking critical resource load.
- Frozen plans are protected instead of reshuffled for every delay.
- Execution works from one priority mechanism.
- Merchandising and pre-production are controlled through workflow tickets.
- Critical operations such as laundry are scheduled with constraint logic.
- Dashboards and review routines sustain the new behavior.

## 3. Business Context Presented

The deck begins by positioning Vector Consulting Group as an implementation-oriented consulting partner. Vector presents itself as a firm focused on operations flow management, not just advisory recommendations. Its engagement model emphasizes:

- Build-operate-transfer.
- Implementation and change management.
- Software tools customized from Vector Digital Labs assets.
- Fixed and variable fee linked to performance improvement.
- Sustenance through SOPs, KRAs, champions, process audits, dashboards, and training.

For Eratex, the deck frames the business need around sustainable sales and gross contribution growth. It states that sales and gross contribution have grown only marginally, while costs keep increasing. Therefore, Eratex needs to become more attractive to current and new customers by becoming a reliable supplier.

The deck defines the customer need as:

- On-time delivery.
- In-full quantities.
- Delivery within customer-expected lead time.
- Reliable quality as a hygiene requirement.

## 4. Current Performance Reality

The presentation highlights a gap between apparent delivery performance and true customer reliability.

### 4.1 On-Time Performance Looks Good Only Under Lenient Measurement

The deck states that OTD is around 95% when Eratex allows a 1-2 day leeway against the ex-factory date.

However, when in-full performance is included, the reliability drops by about 15-20 percentage points.

### 4.2 In-Full Performance Is a Major Gap

The deck states that every month around 15-25% of orders are short-shipped. This turns a seemingly acceptable on-time measure into a weaker OTIF measure.

The presentation estimates OTIF at around 75-80% when both on-time and in-full are considered.

### 4.3 First-Commitment Reliability Is Lower

The deck further argues that if performance is measured against originally committed dates, rather than revised dates, OTIF would be around 65-75%.

This distinction matters because customers experience reliability against the first commitment, not against the revised internal plan.

### 4.4 Lead Times Are Long

The deck states that almost 30-40% of orders experience lead time greater than 130 days, excluding orders with shorter lead-time expectations.

This is important because a preferred supplier must not only deliver on time but must also deliver within the customer's expected lead-time window.

## 5. Diagnosed Causes of Unreliability

The deck organizes unreliability into four broad causes:

1. Failure to meet Full Kit Date (FKD).
2. Failure to meet Planned Cut Date (PCD).
3. Planning-related overload of resources.
4. Execution-related failures.

### 5.1 FKD Failures

Full Kit Date failures are attributed to delays in:

- Fabric.
- Trims.
- PPS or garment test sample approval.

The deck states that FKD OTIF has improved and is around 80-90%, but it is still calculated on revised FKD dates. It also notes that some FKD dates themselves undergo changes.

The deeper issue is not only whether FKD is missed. It is that FKD-related activities are spread across many people, trackers, communications, and dependencies. The deck describes this as:

- Lack of visibility.
- Coordination delays.
- Different priorities across departments.
- Incomplete handovers.
- Reactive decision-making.
- Person-dependent follow-up.

### 5.2 PCD Failures and PCD Instability

The deck initially notes that only about 5-10% of orders may appear to miss PCD. But it then argues that this measure hides instability because PCD dates themselves undergo repeated revisions.

The slide examples show PCD dates shifting week to week through:

- Pull-aheads.
- Postponements.
- Completions.
- Shifts beyond normal tolerance.

The deck states that failures in adherence to PCD are much more than 10% when PCD movement is considered.

The root cause is that pre-production operates under uncertainty. Teams try to do everything as soon as possible because they do not know what will become urgent. This creates:

- Priority conflict.
- Resource overload.
- High WIP in preparatory activities.
- Delayed task completion.
- Expediting pressure.
- PCD misses.

### 5.3 Resource Overload and Planning Mismatch

The deck argues that the current planning process can level-load one department while unknowingly overloading another.

It identifies critical resources across the garment manufacturing flow:

- Cutting.
- Relaxation.
- Embroidery/printing.
- Sewing.
- Laundry.
- Finishing.
- RM store.
- FG warehouse.

The presentation highlights laundry as a key constraint area. It shows week-wise laundry wet-process load exceeding available capacity in some weeks, while other resources may appear level-loaded. It also shows sewing, cutting, and finishing load imbalances.

The point is that a plan cannot be considered valid if it only checks one department. It must check all critical resources and sub-resources.

### 5.4 Execution Instability

The deck identifies production execution instability as a major cause of loss:

- Frequent sewing line changes.
- Sewing start date changes.
- Orders spread across more lines than desired.
- Unplanned changeovers.
- Lower sewing efficiency.
- Backlog formation.
- Capacity loss.

The extracted data shows high week-to-week changes in sewing line assignments and sewing start dates. The deck states that this causes loss in output and forces expediting actions.

### 5.5 Laundry Execution Problems

Laundry is treated as a special critical operation because it has:

- Variable routing by CMT/order.
- Shifting bottleneck work centers.
- Both piece-based dry processes and lot-based wet processes.
- Batch formation constraints.
- Customer-machine preferences.
- Changeover concerns.
- Lot availability dependencies.

The deck states that on any day laundry has about 25% idle time, and the laundry shift plan is not adhered to around 50% of the time. Reasons include lots not being available at wash or lot-making, incorrect WIP lot estimation, machine downtime, and sequencing problems.

## 6. Operational Consequences

The presentation links the above causes to business losses.

### 6.1 Firefighting and Management Bandwidth Loss

Frequent plan changes force merchandising, PPIC, production, procurement, and management teams into daily follow-up and expediting. The deck repeatedly frames this as a loss of bandwidth that prevents leadership from focusing on quality and process improvement.

### 6.2 Overtime, Outsourcing, Air Freight, and Penalties

Postponements and execution instability create expediting pressure. The deck shows overtime costs, outsourcing costs, and airfreight/penalty costs as consequences of unreliable flow.

### 6.3 Excess Finished Goods Inventory

Preponement creates FG inventory buildup. Producing early may protect shipment dates but creates working capital lock and operational clutter.

### 6.4 Fabric and Trim Inventory Lock

The appendix data shows more than 40 days of fabric stock at warehouse, with more than 15 days of stock aged above 45 days. It also notes around 5 days of dead stock, mostly due to customer cancellations.

The deck also notes that POs due 20 days apart are clubbed in the same fabric PO. This suggests procurement batching and early material arrival are being used to protect uncertainty, but at the cost of working capital and ageing.

### 6.5 Capacity Loss

Frequent schedule changes and poor synchronization reduce effective output. The deck presents capacity loss in sewing in million SAM and connects lost time to later expediting.

## 7. Project Objective Defined by the Deck

The deck states the project objective as:

Eratex must establish system-driven operations while achieving high OTIF above 95% consistently.

This objective includes:

- Reliability in operations.
- On-time, every time.
- Delivery on originally committed dates.
- Delivery within customer-accepted lead times.
- Elimination of firefighting.
- Release of merchandising, operations, and preparatory management bandwidth.
- Minimization of overtime and wasted production capacity.
- Minimization of excess inventory.

The deck is therefore proposing not just a scheduling system, but an operating model that stabilizes the full order flow from commitment to production completion.

## 8. Proposed Solution Direction

The deck's proposed solution has several connected elements.

### 8.1 Plan the Business in Zones

The deck divides the horizon before ex-factory into zones:

- Future zone.
- Volatile zone.
- Firm zone.

The exact boundaries vary in the slides based on fabric lead time, but the conceptual rule is clear:

- In the future zone, the system should predict a reliable delivery week based on load ahead of critical resources.
- In the volatile zone, merchandising should get order confirmation and retain flexibility to reschedule or prioritize strategic customers.
- In the firm zone, orders entering the zone should have fixed due dates and should be scheduled on lines.

The purpose is to avoid treating every order change equally. The closer an order is to execution, the more governance and discipline are needed.

### 8.2 Freeze the Plan Within a Scheduling Horizon

The deck argues that every delay should not trigger a plan change. Instead:

- The plan should be frozen within a defined window.
- Delays after freeze should be managed in execution.
- OTIF should be protected while avoiding continuous reshuffling.

This is a major conceptual shift. The current approach tries to manage delays through planning changes and execution changes. The proposed model separates:

- Delays to be absorbed or handled in execution.
- Changes that genuinely require replanning.

### 8.3 Build an Error-Free Frozen Plan

The deck says a frozen plan will fail if it is based on ad hoc commitments or leaves no capacity buffer for shocks.

An error-free frozen plan requires:

- Weekly load visibility for all critical resources.
- Under-loading and over-loading prevention by resource sub-category.
- System-suggested ex-factory or delivery date.
- Load mapping across all critical resources, not only departments.
- Resource sub-category visibility.

### 8.4 Define Critical Resource Categories and Sub-Categories

The proposed model requires each order to be mapped against critical resource categories and sub-categories.

Examples from the deck:

- Sewing categories: basic, cargo, chino, jackets.
- Sewing sub-categories by customer-product-line group, such as AMEO Basic, GU Basic, AT Basic, CK Basic, Levis Basic.
- Laundry categories: wet process, acid wash, moon wash, laser dry process, manual dry process.
- Laundry sub-categories: Tonello 125 kg, Tonello 150 kg, Brongo, Yilmax, Tolkar, Ramson, PP Spray Booth, Mannequin.

The reason for sub-categories is practical: a basic 5-pocket order cannot be loaded against all sewing capacity if in reality it can run only on a subset of lines. Similarly, wash orders can run only on certain machine groups.

### 8.5 Define Routes for Articles

The proposed system must define routings for all articles, across processes such as:

- Cutting.
- Relaxation.
- Embroidery/printing.
- Sewing.
- Laundry.
- Finishing.
- FG store.

The route determines which critical resources are loaded, in what sequence, and with what capacity requirement.

### 8.6 Calculate Load Using SMV, Quantity, Resource Capacity, and Efficiency

The deck gives examples of load calculations:

- Sewing load = order quantity x garment SAM / daily capacity of the allocated sewing sub-category.
- Daily sewing capacity depends on number of lines, shift hours, people per line, and efficiency.
- Laundry load depends on route, operation time, lot count or piece count, machine count, and available hours.

The deck uses examples such as:

- 7,353 pieces at 27.2 minutes per garment mapped to AMEO Basic lines.
- Laundry loads calculated for laser machines, wash operations, Tonello, Tolkar, Vaportech, and other resources.

The implication is that planning must be capacity-constrained, not only date-driven.

### 8.7 Use an Order Loading Tool to Select Delivery Week

The proposed order loading tool takes inputs such as:

- SMV.
- Route.
- Laundry routing information.
- Customer-demanded week.
- Current load chart across critical resources.

It then checks whether the demanded week is feasible. If not, it suggests alternate weeks.

The user iterates until a favorable load scenario is reached.

This tool becomes the basis for reliable delivery week quotation and order acceptance.

### 8.8 Use Dynamic Capacity Masters

Capacity should not be fixed once and reused blindly. The deck highlights:

- Sewing capacity varies by 7-hour, 8-hour, or 9-hour shifts.
- Users must choose the shift timing for the specified week.
- Laundry capacity is calculated at a 21-hour x 7-day level.
- Holidays and pre-planned maintenance must be excluded.
- Capacity buffers should be applied based on past work-center performance, breakdowns, and unplanned maintenance.

The planning system must therefore maintain time-phased, resource-specific, reality-adjusted capacities.

### 8.9 Use FastReact in Auto Mode with Vector Flow

The deck proposes interaction between Vector Flow and FastReact:

- Vector Flow receives exact sewing load and sewing line end date daily from FastReact.
- Datatex supplies inputs such as unscheduled confirmed orders.
- Vector Flow calculates which sub-CCR to run, number of lines, and which lines.
- FastReact provides the latest plan and order-line allocation.
- Vector Flow applies logic such as learning curve, days of run required, sewing start date, and sewing end date.
- Vector Flow updates the FastReact plan.

The deck explicitly argues that FastReact should function in auto mode as an automated visualization tool using Vector Flow inputs. It warns that manual overrides in FastReact will create differences in load and sew-end dates received by Vector Flow, causing due dates to keep changing daily and preventing a fixed plan.

This is important for future tool planning: the proposed architecture treats FastReact as a planning visualization/scheduling board, while Vector Flow carries important logic for allocation and calculation.

### 8.10 Manage Execution Through a Single Priority System

The deck proposes a buffer-management priority system using colors:

- Black: highest priority; buffer fully consumed or overrun.
- Red: high priority; more than two-thirds buffer consumed.
- Yellow: medium priority; one-third to two-thirds buffer consumed.
- Green: low priority; less than one-third buffer consumed.
- White: lowest priority; no buffer consumed.

In production, the same priority logic is framed as Black >> Red > Yellow > Green.

Each work center should work according to the same priority signal. The priority is based on elapsed time against the production lead-time buffer, not on local discretion.

### 8.11 Institutionalize Daily Flow Meetings

The deck proposes daily flow meetings to control elapsed time after release.

Characteristics:

- High frequency: daily.
- Fixed time.
- Fixed place.
- Fixed participants.
- Conducted by a Daily Flow Meeting champion.
- Focused on expediting Black and Red orders.
- Focused on resolution, not blame.
- Tracks DFM remarks for elapsed time.

The meeting covers:

- Full-kit completion and FKD adherence.
- Pre-production completion and PCD adherence.
- Production alignment across planning and production.

The deck also notes that multi-line orders require monitoring of basket completion and the elapsed time between first line and last line completion.

### 8.12 Add a Daily Scheduler for Laundry Operations

The deck identifies laundry as too complex for manual daily scheduling.

A good laundry schedule must:

- Keep bottlenecks from starving.
- Minimize CMT changes within a shift to reduce setups.
- Adhere to ex-factory date priorities.
- Account for time and capacity buffers at constraint machines.
- Account for batch formation.
- Sequence machine-wise, order-wise, and operation-wise.

The scheduler inputs include:

- Constraint machine identification.
- CMT-wise route data.
- Changeover time mapping.
- Pending orders with priority.
- Stage-wise status of all orders on the floor.
- Capacity calendar.

The scheduler should schedule batches one after another, group same CMT-washes where possible, use pre-selected machine types, and maintain sufficient time gaps between successive activities.

## 9. Workflow Automation for Merchandising and Pre-Production

The deck argues that execution control must be added in merchandising and pre-production if Eratex wants high OTIF.

The proposed system here is described as TMS or Workflow Automation System (WAS).

### 9.1 Problem With Current Workflow

Current merchandising and pre-production work is described as fragmented across:

- Emails.
- WhatsApp.
- Phone calls.
- Online platforms.
- Excel trackers.
- Memory-based follow-up.

This creates:

- Missed triggers.
- Incomplete handovers.
- Multiple priority signals.
- Delayed task completion.
- Parallel systems.
- Resource bandwidth loss.
- Expediting.

### 9.2 Single Platform for Activities

The solution proposes that all activities of a project be pre-loaded on one platform.

For each project:

- Activity sequence is predefined.
- Conditions to start activities are predefined.
- Open activities are visible to each user across all projects.
- Workflows are defined by customer, fabric type, and garment type.
- Sub-flows can be selected automatically from order attributes or manually by users.

### 9.3 Ticket Opening Logic

A ticket opens when a resource is expected to do something to complete an activity.

The deck defines several rules:

- A ticket should open only when all prerequisite activities are closed.
- A ticket should not open if the resource cannot start working on it.
- If a task has a definite time trigger, the system should assign a start date and open the ticket only on that date.
- Opening work too early wastes resource capacity.

### 9.4 Ticket Splitting Logic

Activities may be split into multiple tickets when consecutive subtasks have significant elapsed time or require handoff. For example, cut head end may be split into relax shrinkage, marking shrinkage, and related subtasks.

Activities should not be split when consecutive tasks are handled by the same team/resource and elapsed time between them is negligible.

### 9.5 Ticket Closure Logic

A ticket closes only when the closure criteria are met.

The deck rejects open-ended yes/no closure. Instead, each ticket should have mandatory closure inputs such as:

- Checklist values.
- Numbers.
- Dates.
- Text fields.
- File uploads.

For procurement PO creation, examples include:

- Ex-mill date.
- PO number.
- Mandatory fields.

The system should not allow closure without required information.

### 9.6 API Integration and Data Reuse

The deck says existing Eratex system data should flow into TMS through APIs to avoid manual re-entry.

Examples include:

- Customer name.
- Quantity.
- Dates.
- Style number.
- PO-related data.

The deck explicitly states the system should be able to read and use data already available in Eratex's other IT systems.

### 9.7 Centralized Document Repository

Tickets should allow relevant file uploads. Uploaded documents remain accessible to subsequent users until project completion.

This addresses the problem of documents being passed through email, WhatsApp, or physical copies.

### 9.8 Intermediate Checkpoints

For critical activities requiring multiple inputs, the deck proposes a check ticket. The check ticket validates whether necessary and correct information is available.

If information is incomplete or incorrect:

- The previous ticket is reopened.
- The next ticket does not open.

This prevents downstream teams from starting work with incomplete information.

### 9.9 SLA and Project Health Priority

The deck defines ticket SLA as a function of:

- Touch time.
- Nominal queue length.

A ticket becomes due only after SLA breach, and due tickets take higher priority.

But the deck argues SLA priority alone is insufficient. Ticket priority must also reflect project health and proximity to the next milestone, such as FKD or PCD.

The project priority uses buffer penetration:

Buffer Penetration (%) = workflow delay days / buffer.

This creates a single project priority system across all open projects.

### 9.10 Project Initiation

The deck notes that many order-related activities begin before ERP status changes. Therefore, the project should start without waiting for manual status update.

The proposed TMS should process new-order triggers received in emails and create project-initiation tickets. The merchandising team then furnishes additional information and chooses or validates the workflow route.

## 10. Ongoing Improvement and Sustenance Model

The deck includes a POOGI element: Process of Ongoing Improvement.

Whenever an order turns Red, the system should capture all contributing reasons. These reasons are tabulated and analyzed monthly through Pareto analysis to launch improvement projects.

Examples of improvement projects include:

- Reducing setups.
- Quality improvement.
- Reducing machine breakdowns.
- Other lean improvement actions.

The sustenance model includes:

- IT tools.
- Documented SOPs and KRAs.
- Training champions from each functional area.
- Reviews and dashboards.
- Process sustenance audits.

The proposed dashboard hierarchy covers:

- Overall OTIF.
- Lead time.
- FG spread.
- Merchandising priority management.
- Pre-production priority management.
- Cutting WIP.
- Sewing.
- Laundry.

The dashboards are intended to detect violations and support top-management review rather than relying on manual follow-up.

## 13. What the Presentation Is Really Proposing

The presentation is proposing an integrated manufacturing flow-control system, not a standalone scheduling UI.

At its core, the proposal is:

1. Stabilize customer commitment by quoting dates based on real resource load.
2. Protect the plan through future, volatile, and firm-zone governance.
3. Make the frozen plan credible by checking all critical resource sub-categories.
4. Use Vector Flow to calculate load, capacity, line allocation, and due-date feasibility.
5. Use FastReact as an automated visualization and plan-holding tool, avoiding manual override drift.
6. Use a unified priority mechanism during execution, based on buffer penetration.
7. Add a daily flow meeting to resolve Black and Red priorities.
8. Add a constraint scheduler for laundry and other critical operations.
9. Add TMS/WAS to control merchandising and pre-production tasks before FKD and PCD.
10. Sustain through dashboards, SOPs, champions, and POOGI.

## 16. Final Synthesis

The deck presents Eratex's core issue as unreliable flow, not simply poor scheduling. Orders change dates, materials and approvals miss readiness milestones, critical resources are overloaded unevenly, sewing is reshuffled frequently, laundry is manually scheduled under complex constraints, and teams compensate through overtime, outsourcing, inventory, air freight, and management escalation.

The proposed solution is a system-driven flow-management operating model. It starts from capacity-based order commitment, continues through frozen-zone planning and resource-level load checks, and then controls execution using buffer priorities, daily meetings, and workflow automation. The tool vision combines Vector Flow, FastReact, Datatex inputs, TMS/WAS, laundry scheduling, dashboards, and ongoing improvement routines.

For future Eratex planning, the key takeaway is that the intended solution must be designed as a closed-loop planning and execution system:

- Commit only against realistic critical-resource capacity.
- Freeze the plan at the right horizon.
- Use execution priorities to absorb delays rather than constantly replan.
- Control FKD and PCD through workflow tickets and milestone health.
- Schedule critical operations such as laundry with constraint logic.
- Measure and improve through dashboards and reason-code Pareto.

This synthesis should be used as a bridge between the presentation narrative and formal product planning for the Eratex scheduling and operations platform.
